One row per target, because only the target barrier changes. The two stops are identical across all three rows, so racing each target against them tells you which profit target is actually worth aiming at on this contract — a single race against one hand-picked target cannot. Every row sums to 100%: each of the 2,000 paths is attributed once, to whichever barrier it reaches first.

Read what this contract is telling you. The premium stop sits at $2.08; the stock stop is worth $1.09 by the time it is reached. The premium stop is tighter, so it fires first on almost every losing path — the stock stop reaches its level first only 1–2% of the time, at any target. Arming the stock stop on this contract means the position is already down more than 60% before it can trigger. That is a fact you cannot get from any card on the page today.

Median days are conditional on that outcome — "~11d to T30%" is the median over the 31% of paths that got there first, not over all paths.

Only the bold column is a measurement. The stock-stop risk is premium − Black-Scholes(stop), which depends on strike, spot at the stop, time and IV independently — so it genuinely varies contract to contract. The premium-stop column is greyed because it reduces exactly to your target % ÷ your stop % (0.30 ÷ 0.60 = 0.50×) — the same three numbers for every contract you will ever look at. It is shown for completeness and labelled so it can never be mistaken for a discriminator. This is the trap that killed two earlier R:R designs.

Both legs priced at the simulation's own median time to that barrier, not at a fixed half of the contract's life. On the old half-life basis the T30% row reads 0.32× instead of 0.38× — the difference is pure time-decay you were charged for days you would never have held.

Reaching for a bigger target makes this contract worse, not better: the reward grows from $1.04 to $2.60, but the share of paths that get there collapses from 48% to 12% while you sit through more premium-stop hits. T20% is the least bad row and it is still negative. A negative edge is not a veto — it says this contract needs a different strike, a wider premium stop, or a closer target to be worth $5.20.

The "Neither" contribution is not a guess: it is the mean value of those paths at expiry, from the same simulation, minus the premium paid. Written as a signed term — a path that reached no barrier can still end above what you paid.

Assumes implied volatility is unchanged when a barrier is reached. On a long call that is pessimistic at the stop and optimistic at the target: a real fear bid would leave more premium than shown. On the reference contract, IV rising 5 points cuts the stop loss from 71% of premium to 36%.

Where CAUTION starts — re-derived for the new ruler

The gate never blocks and never paints red: two states, and the raw percentage always shows so a high-conviction trade can be taken anyway. The only question a threshold answers is how much of the premium must survive the stop for the contract to count as one you can afford to be wrong on.

The holding-time YAML — one factor per preset

Your scan offers seven horizon presets but the scorer only knows three. Today Quarterly, Semi-annual and Annual all collapse into "Position" — so a 200-day hold and a 20-day hold would share a fallback. The factors must key on the preset, not the scoring horizon.

reprice_horizon:
  # How long the trade is assumed to run when a level is repriced.
  # Priority: 1) the simulation's own median days to that barrier
  #           2) fallback_days below, when the simulation returns nothing
  #              (dte < 1, or zero paths reached the barrier)
  source: simulation_median
  fallback_days:
    Intraday:    1      # hold 0–2d
    Swing:       7      # hold 5–15d
    Position:   14      # hold 15–45d
    Quarterly:  25      # hold 45–60d
    SemiAnnual: 45      # hold 90–105d
    Annual:     80      # hold 165–200d

  # Manual date selection ("Custom"), and any fallback that must not
  # exceed the contract itself:
  custom:
    rule: fraction_of_hold        # fraction_of_hold | nearest_preset
    fraction: 0.50                # median hold = (holdMin + holdMax) / 2, halved
  clamp:
    min_days: 1
    max_fraction_of_dte: 0.75     # never reprice past 3/4 of the contract's life

Why fraction_of_hold and not nearest_preset: a manual 30–35 day range sits between Position and Quarterly, and snapping either way moves the repriced loss by real money. Deriving from the range you actually typed — midpoint of hold, halved — is both continuous and explainable. The existing scoringHorizonForHold() already sets the precedent for deriving from the typed range rather than snapping.

Why the clamp: without max_fraction_of_dte an Annual preset on a 30-day contract would reprice at 80 days — past expiry — and every level would price at intrinsic value only.
